How to invoice international clients

Working with a client abroad changes three things on your invoice: the currency, the sales tax and the way you get paid. Here is a practical checklist for freelancers based in Canada, the UK or the US who sell services to clients in other countries.

  1. Agree the currency before you start — Put the currency in your quote or contract: your own (you avoid exchange risk) or the client's (easier for them to approve). Always write the currency code — USD, CAD, GBP, EUR, AUD — because '$' alone is ambiguous between US, Canadian and Australian dollars.
  2. Identify the client and keep proof of their status — Write the client's full legal name and address. For a business client, ask for their VAT or tax number if they have one and keep it with your records: sales tax treatment of services often depends on whether the customer is a business and where it is located.
  3. Canada: services to non-residents are often zero-rated — If you are registered for GST/HST, many services supplied to a non-resident client are zero-rated (taxable at 0%), so you charge no GST/HST but still show the supply on your return. There are exceptions, for example services relating to real property in Canada or services to an individual who is in Canada when the service is performed. Check the CRA's guidance for your type of service.
  4. UK: business clients abroad are usually outside the scope of UK VAT — Under the general rule for business-to-business services, the place of supply is where the customer belongs, so you don't charge UK VAT to an overseas business client; note on the invoice that the supply is outside the scope of UK VAT and that the customer accounts for any VAT in their country (reverse charge). Some services follow special rules, and sales to consumers abroad are treated differently.
  5. US: no federal VAT, sales tax is state-level — The US has no national VAT or GST. Sales tax is set by each state and depends on what you sell and where the customer is; services delivered to clients outside the US are generally not subject to state sales tax, but check your state's rules for your type of service.
  6. Make international payment simple and state who pays the fees — Give complete payment details: account name, IBAN or account number, SWIFT/BIC, or a PayPal or payment link. Say who covers transfer and conversion fees (for example 'All bank fees are paid by the client') so you receive the full amount.
  7. Record the income in your own currency — Your tax return is in your home currency. Convert each payment using a rate your tax authority accepts — in Canada, for example, the Bank of Canada rate for the day or the annual average rate — and keep the original invoice plus the amount actually received.
