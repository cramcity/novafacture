How to charge late fees on an invoice

Late fees encourage clients to pay on time, but they only work if they are agreed in advance and written clearly. Here is how to set them up properly, with the main rules in the UK, the US and Canada.

  1. Agree the fee before you start — Put your late fee in your contract, quote or terms and conditions, and get the client to accept them before the work starts. A fee added only after the invoice is overdue is hard to enforce.
  2. State it on every invoice — Write the due date and the fee next to it, for example: 'Payment due within 30 days. Late payments incur interest of 1.5% per month (18% per year).'
  3. UK: you can claim statutory interest — For business-to-business debts, the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998 lets you claim interest at 8% above the Bank of England base rate, plus fixed compensation of £40 (debt under £1,000), £70 (£1,000 to £9,999.99) or £100 (£10,000 or more).
  4. US: check your state's limits — There is no single federal rule. States cap interest rates and some limit late fees, so keep your rate reasonable (1% to 1.5% per month is common) and check your state's rules.
  5. Canada: show the annual rate — Under the federal Interest Act, if you state interest only as a monthly rate without its yearly equivalent, you can generally recover no more than 5% per year. Write both, for example '1.5% per month (18% per year)'.
  6. Remind before you charge — Send a polite reminder a few days after the due date first. Many late payments are oversights, and a friendly reminder keeps the relationship intact; apply the fee if the invoice stays unpaid.
